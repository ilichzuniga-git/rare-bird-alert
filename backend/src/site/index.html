Finding birds
01

The map and the Rarities list

Every pin is a rare bird reported in Los Angeles or Orange County. A pin's colour is how rare the bird is, and the rarest show their names.

Under the map is the Rarities list. The large card at the top is the rarest bird, and the rest follow newest first. Drag the list up to read more of it, or down to see more of the map.

How rare is rare?

The rating counts how often the species has ever been recorded in the county (research-grade iNaturalist observations).

02

Three ways to look

  • This week shows birds reported since Sunday, with a count.
  • Last 30 days shows everything still on file.
  • Near me sorts birds by distance from you and marks your spot on the map. It needs location access, and your location never leaves the phone.
03

Narrow it down

The pills under the views pick a source: All sources, eBird or iNaturalist.

The search box at the top finds species or places. It matches the start of words, so rail finds Ridgway's Rail but not "River Trail".

04New in 1.4

Birds at sea

Some rarities are seen from boats on pelagic trips, more than 2 km offshore. Few people go on those, so they're hidden at first.

The 🚤 At sea pill shows how many there are. While they're hidden it's red and crossed out. Tap it to show them, and tap again to hide them. Islands such as Catalina count as land.

On a bird
05

A bird's page

Tap any pin or card. The map zooms to where it's been reported, and the dashed circle is the area the reports cover.

  • The day strip covers the last week. Green means it was reported that day, and red means people only dipped.
  • Refound or Dipped: if you go, tell other birders whether you saw it. Reports are anonymous.
  • Directions, Notes (what observers wrote) and a link to the eBird report.
  • The report chips: tap one to see its exact spot.

06New in 1.4

Seen at sea

An offshore bird's page says so. It has no Directions or trip button, because you can't drive to it.

Going birding
07

Plan a trip

  • On a bird's page, tap the car button to save it to your trip.
  • The car button at the top right opens the trip. The number on it is how many birds are saved.
  • Stops are put in driving order from where you are. Directions opens the whole route in your maps app (on iPhone, you pick Apple Maps or Google Maps).
  • Swipe a bird sideways to remove it.
Staying in the loop
08

Rare-bird alerts

The app checks for new reports every 10 minutes. When there are new ones, you get one alert that names them. Birds you can drive to come first, and offshore ones come last, marked "(at sea)".

Tap an alert to open the bird. If it named several, they're listed first under Just reported.

Since 1.5, the app icon also shows how many alerts came in since you last opened the app. Opening it clears the number.

Tip An alert gone too fast? On iPhone go to Settings → Notifications → Birder's Best Friend → Banner Style → Persistent, and banners will stay until you swipe them away. Missed alerts also wait on the Lock Screen and in Notification Center. On Android, they wait in the pull-down shade.

09New in 1.5

About, and this guide

The logo button at the top left opens About. The teal card at the top brings you back to this guide: Take the tour or What's new. After each update the app also shows a short "What's new" card once.

Below it, About explains the sources:

  • eBird (Cornell Lab of Ornithology): notable sightings, observer notes, hotspots.
  • iNaturalist: research-grade rare observations and the bird photos.
  • BirdWeather and BirdNET: microphones that back up birds people have already reported.

The app is free and non-commercial, with no accounts and no ads.

Privacy policy · last updated October 4, 2026

Birder's Best Friend is a free, non-commercial app for Android and iPhone that shows rare bird sightings in Los Angeles and Orange Counties. It has no accounts, no ads, and no analytics or tracking. This page explains the little data the app does handle.

What the app collects

  • Refound and dipped reports. When you tap "Refound" or "Dipped", the server stores only which bird it was, which button you tapped, and when. It does not store your location or any identifier for your phone.
  • A push notification token. If you allow notifications, the app registers a token (issued by Expo, and delivered through Google Firebase Cloud Messaging on Android or the Apple Push Notification service on iPhone) with our server so it can send rare-bird alerts. The token identifies the app install for delivery, not you. Next to it the server keeps a count of alerts sent since you last opened the app, for the number on the app's icon; opening the app resets it to zero.

Reports and tokens are not linked to your name, email, or any account; the app never asks for them.

What stays on your phone

The app uses your location only on your device: to sort birds by distance and show your spot on the map in the "Near me" view, and to show how far you are from a bird when you report it. That location is not sent to our server.

Earlier versions of the app sent your coordinates with each report. Since October 1, 2026 the server discards them if an older version still sends them, and the locations that earlier reports had stored have been deleted.

How it's used

Only to run the app: reports help show whether a bird is still being seen, and push tokens are used to deliver alerts. We do not sell, rent, or share this data, and we don't use it for advertising.

Services the app talks to

Like any app that loads content from the internet, your phone connects directly to these services, which see your IP address under their own privacy policies:

  • iNaturalist — bird photos
  • OpenStreetMap — map tiles
  • unpkg — the Leaflet map library
  • Expo and Google Firebase (Android) or Apple (iPhone) — delivering push notifications

Links you tap (eBird pages, directions) open in your browser or maps app and are governed by those services.

Security and retention

All data is sent over encrypted HTTPS connections. Our server briefly keeps IP addresses in memory (for at most an hour) to limit spam and abuse; the app never saves them to its database. Push tokens are deleted when they stop working (for example, after you uninstall the app). Reports are kept to show each bird's history.

Your choices

  • Deny or revoke location access in your phone's settings; the app still works.
  • Turn off notifications in your phone's settings, or uninstall the app, to stop alerts.

Children

The app is not directed at children under 13 and does not knowingly collect their data.
